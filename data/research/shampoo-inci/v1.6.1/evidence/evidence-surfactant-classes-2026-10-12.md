# Evidence: surfactant class mapping for four unlisted ingredients (C3 table)

Date: 2026-10-12. Scope: cosmetic cleansing-strength classification only (standard v1.6, C1-C6). No medical claims.
Method: web/literature search, Europe PMC abstracts, Google Patents full text, supplier pages. Marketing claims ("reinigt mild", "ultra-mild") were NOT used as evidence for class assignment; they are listed only so the team can see what they rest on.

## How to read the confidence labels

- Source grade: A = peer-reviewed / regulatory (CIR); B = patent with test data or supplier data with a stated number; C = supplier marketing, database listing, forum.
- "Seen" = I opened the document. "Snippet only" = the claim came from a search-engine summary and I could not open the source (paywall, 403, certificate error). Treat snippet-only items as unverified.
- Overall point for the whole file: **the public literature contains almost no head-to-head zein, collagen-swelling, TEWL or sebum-removal data for any of these four ingredients.** Every mapping below is a repeatability convention backed by indirect evidence, not a potency finding. C1/C3-C6 already say this; the recommendation is to keep the N-ALT mechanism doing the work.

## 1. Sodium Methyl 2-Sulfolaurate and Disodium 2-Sulfolaurate

### Chemistry
- Alpha-sulfo fatty acid derivatives of lauric/C12-C18 acid. The sulfonate group sits on carbon 2, next to the carbonyl. The methyl ester salt is RCH(SO3Na)COOCH3; the disalt is RCH(SO3Na)COONa. Commercial Stepan ALPHA-STEP PC-48 is a blend of both (INCI "Sodium Methyl 2-Sulfolaurate (and) Disodium 2-Sulfolaurate"), C12-C18 chain distribution, about 37-40% active, 10% solution pH 5-7 (Surfachem page seen; Stepan/Tilley/Knowde figures snippet only).
- BASF Texapon SFA is disodium 2-sulfolaurate (CosIng ref 55875 per SpecialChem listing). A BASF patent example describes a technical SFA as about 74% disodium 2-sulfolaurate, 13% sodium laurate, 11% sodium sulfate, 2% water (snippet only; source US patent family on SFA/CAPB blends). The pure disalt has poor water solubility (Henkel EP0281027: "limited solubility", opacifiers needed; another BASF-family patent snippet gives 0.7% for the C14 disalt at 20 degC). That is why shampoo products use it with a betaine or as the methyl ester blend.
- Not a sulfate ester: the sulfur is bound to carbon (sulfonate), so it is not an alkyl sulfate. It is also not an alpha-olefin sulfonate (no double bond, ester/carboxylate present). It is the reverse arrangement of a sulfoacetate (which is a fatty alcohol ester of sulfoacetic acid): both carry sulfonate plus carbonyl.
- Typical use and function: primary or co-primary anionic in "sulfate-free" shampoo and body wash; high foam, hard-water tolerance, low viscosity response, claimed to enhance cationic polymer deposition (supplier claims). Stepan starter formulation 1112 (Economy Sulfate-Free Shampoo) uses 13.5% PC-48 with 10% AMPHOSOL CDB-HP (snippet only; the formulation PDF could not be opened because of a certificate error).

### Mildness and irritation evidence
| Evidence | Result | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Stepan product bulletin comparative irritation table (days until a score of 1; higher = milder; test protocol not retrievable) | PC-48 5.2; SLES 3-mole 5.07; sodium cocoyl isethionate 4.27; SLS 2.7; dry-patch control 13.5. Also: neat material non-irritating in vitro; 1% active mild in repeated soap-chamber test. | B- (manufacturer data, snippet only, method unknown) |
| BASF Texapon SFA marketing | "ultra-mild", "tear-free", gentle on skin and eyes | C (no numbers found; no zein data found) |
| Lion Corp patent WO93/25646 (seen, background section) | States that alpha-sulfo fatty acid methyl ester salts "are irritable to the human body" and therefore not widely used in skin-contact products; invention targets a lower-irritation derivative | B- (competitor patent background; no numbers; older, methyl ester only) |
| Patents citing zein on methyl ester sulfonates (laundry context: MES 1.24% vs LAS 2.27% zein) | MES milder than LAS and SLS | B- (snippet only, laundry formulas, not shampoo) |
| Yamaguchi et al. 2014, J Oleo Sci 63:995 (seen, abstract) | Compared alkyl sulfates and fatty acid methyl ester sulfonates, C8-C14, in a reconstructed epidermis. Skin damage depended on chain length via permeation and membrane fluidity. | A, but no MES-versus-SLES ranking available in the abstract |
| Henkel EP0281027 (seen) | Claims better skin feel when 0.1-9% alpha-sulfo fatty acid salts are added; no zein or Draize data | C |

Nothing found: zein number, collagen swelling, TEWL, patch-test, or CIR/SCCS opinion for either INCI name. No independent head-to-head against sodium lauryl sulfoacetate or isethionates.

### Cleansing and sebum removal
- Suppliers state "excellent detergency, foaming and mildness" (Surfachem/Stepan page, seen) with high foam. No sebum-removal or oily-soil data found. For a C3 convention this is acceptable: the class is about repeatability, not measured potency.

### Mapping reasoning
- Argument for "strong anionic, sulfonate type": the standard says unlisted surfactants take the class their chemistry names, and the name says sulfonate. The Lion patent says the methyl ester sulfonate is irritating.
- Argument for "mild anionic": the only quantitative comparison puts it level with SLES-3EO and above SCI, and SCI is already in the mild anionic row. Both big suppliers sell it as a primary surfactant for sulfate-free systems with an amphoteric partner. It has no alkyl sulfate head group.
- Conflict that cannot be resolved with public data: Lion (methyl ester irritating) versus Stepan/BASF (mild). Both are commercial interests.
- **Recommendation:** map to mild anionic, state the assignment in the record, confidence moderate-low, and record `moderate` as the neighbor wherever the result depends on the call. Do not treat the supplier "mild" wording as the reason; the reason is that the sulfonate sits on a carbon next to a carbonyl (sulfoacetate/sulfosuccinate-like), plus one numerical manufacturer comparison that does not rank it worse than SCI.
- If the team wants the conservative branch: classify as strong anionic, ether/sulfonate type only for the specific products where sulfolaurate is the sole anionic and no amphoteric/glucoside buffer is present. In that case C6 gives `moderate` anyway (mild anionic without buffer), so the two readings differ only when a prominent betaine is present (`low` vs `moderate` under C1).
- Follow-up that would settle it cheaply: request Stepan's full PC-48 irritation report and any zein or red-blood-cell data; ask BASF for a zein/RBC value for Texapon SFA.

## 2. Sulfated Castor Oil (INCI "Sulfated Castor Oil", Turkey red oil, CAS 8002-33-3)

### Chemistry
- Castor oil (mostly triglyceride of ricinoleic acid, a C18 fatty acid with a hydroxyl on carbon 12) treated with sulfuric acid and neutralised with NaOH or ammonia; some modern grades are made with SO3 (ResearchGate abstract snippet only). The sulfate ester forms mainly on the secondary hydroxyl, with partial hydrolysis; the product is a mixture of sulfated ricinoleate esters, free ricinoleate/glycerides and unsulfated oil, not one molecule. Database formula C18H32Na2O6S corresponds to the sulforicinoleate fraction.
- Properties: anionic, soluble/dispersible in water giving a milky emulsion, pH about 7-8 (10%), "organically combined sulfate" specification about 6% minimum on one distributor sheet (Parchem, seen); one supplier datasheet gives about 82% active (snippet only). Description from suppliers: low foaming, solubilizer for essential oils, emollient, historical bath-oil dispersant.
- CosIng functions as listed on third-party sites (not the official database): cleansing agent, humectant, surfactant-cleansing, surfactant-emulsifying (snippet only).

### Mildness and irritation evidence
- No CIR report on sulfated castor oil exists in what I could find (the 2007 CIR castor oil/ricinoleate report does not cover the sulfated form, per the search summary; I did not open the full report).
- No zein, patch-test, TEWL or protein-swelling data found.
- PubChem lists GHS notifications for "Castor oil, sulfated": about half of notifiers (523 of 1056) say it does not meet hazard criteria, others notify skin/eye irritation (snippet only). That signals mixed supplier self-classification, not mildness data.
- Ingredient-rating sites and forum posts are not evidence.

### Cleansing and sebum removal
- No data. Descriptions point to dispersing/solubilizing and low foam. One forum user reports it leaves hair feeling coated and stringy as a sole cleanser (grade C, anecdote).

### Mapping reasoning
- Name-based rule would produce "alkyl sulfate, strong anionic", which is the harsh row (C5). That would be a poor read of a secondary sulfate on a hydroxy-fatty glyceride with a large lipid fraction.
- Closest precedent in the standard: C3a (emulsifier-grade C16-C18 sulfates are not a cleansing route). Sulfated castor oil is C18, carries glyceride/lipid mass, and is used as a dispersant and emulsifier, so the same logic applies, but the standard's C3a condition (paired fatty alcohol in the formula) does not literally hold.
- **Recommendation:** "Not a cleansing route and not a buffer" (add as a new named member of that row, with the reasoning above). Confidence low-moderate. The record should state: assigned by function and chemistry (sulfated lipid, dispersing/refatting co-surfactant), no potency evidence. Because the evidence is thin, add the rule that a sulfated oil never counts as a defining anionic and never as a reinforcing route; if it is the only anionic at a prominent position, mark `cleansingStrength` confidence `low` and escalate rather than infer a value.
- In GLYNT DERMA at position 16 it is very likely behind the E3 prominence line anyway (I have not checked the product's fragrance/preservative positions), in which case C4 already removes it from the class decision regardless of this mapping.

## 3. Cocamidopropylamine Oxide

### Chemistry
- Coco-amidopropyl dimethylamine N-oxide: R-CO-NH-(CH2)3-N+(CH3)2-O-. Zwitterionic N-oxide; essentially nonionic-behaving at neutral to alkaline pH and increasingly cationic below about pH 4-5 (general surfactant knowledge; I did not verify the pKa in a primary source this session).
- Function in shampoo: foam booster and stabilizer, viscosity builder with anionics, conditioning/antistatic. Typical level 1-5% as supplied (the lauramine oxide data point from an Elchemy blog, grade C). CIR safe-as-used conclusion covers rinse-off products.

### Mildness and irritation evidence
| Evidence | Result | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| CIR amended report on cocamidopropylamine oxide, Int J Toxicol 2008;27 Suppl 1:55-62 (abstract seen via Europe PMC) | Low toxicity; no sensitization in guinea pig and clinical tests; safe in rinse-off, leave-on needs absorption data. A search summary of the full text (not opened) reports 5% not a primary dermal irritant in rabbits, 7.5% clinical formulation not a sensitizer but some mild irritation reactions, neat material (81.5%) moderately skin and severely eye irritating. | A (abstract); numbers snippet only |
| Herrero, Calabuig, Ríos, Lechuga 2025 (preprints.org 10.20944/preprints202512.1118.v1; published Cosmetics 2026, 13:17) (abstract seen) | SLS + lauryl dimethylamine oxide (LDAO) blends at six ratios: all blends lower CMC, higher foam, and lower zein-based protein denaturation than SLS; denaturation lower than the sum of the components. 70:30 blend CMC 0.755 vs 2.913 mmol/L for SLS (snippet). In vitro only. | A- (in vitro; different amine oxide; not yet independently replicated) |
| Lechuga et al. 2025, ACS Omega 10:55868 (abstract seen) | Zein number data on 20 commercial surfactants and mixtures: anionic + nonionic mixtures less irritating than the individual anionics; HLB, concentration and ionic character explain about 80% of variance. Individual surfactants not named in the abstract. | A- |
| US 4033895 (stearyl dimethylamine oxide in SLS shampoo), snippet only | Adding the amine oxide made an SLS/ZPT shampoo markedly less irritating to scalp; mechanism unknown | C+ (old patent) |
| Alley et al. 2026, Dermatitis (abstract seen) | LDAO patch tested in 766 patients: 3.79% positive, 27.6% of those clinically relevant (mostly dish and hand soaps); cross-reactivity with the CAPB family is significant | A- (allergy, not cleansing strength; shows it is not allergen-neutral) |

Nothing found: zein, RBC or HET-CAM values for cocamidopropylamine oxide itself, or a head-to-head with CAPB and SLES.

### Cleansing and sebum removal
- Amine oxides are cleaning agents in their own right (supplier tables, grade C) but at shampoo levels act mainly as foam stabilizer and mixed-micelle partner. No sebum-removal data.

### Mapping reasoning
- Booster reading: alkanolamides (Cocamide MEA) are in the "neither route nor buffer" row because they boost foam and viscosity without a documented mildening mixed-micelle effect.
- Buffer reading: amine oxides pair with anionics into mixed micelles (lower CMC) and reduce zein denaturation versus the anionic alone; the amidopropyl variant is closer in head group to CAPB than to the alkanolamides. Direct evidence is for LDAO, not the amidopropyl compound.
- **Recommendation:** amphoteric buffer, counted as a buffer only when prominent (C4), confidence low-moderate, with the neighbor (no buffer) named wherever the buffer is the deciding element (for example a sulfate chassis whose only buffer is the amine oxide: `moderate` with N-ALT `strong`). Do not treat it as a stand-alone cleansing route.
- Caveat for the engine: the finding does not say the amine oxide is as effective a buffer as a betaine or amphoacetate. It says the direction is the same.

## 4. Sodium Laureth-8 Sulfate (high-ethoxylate ether sulfate)

### Chemistry
- C12 alkyl chain, average 8 mol ethylene oxide, sulfate head group. Commercial SLES is mostly 1-3 EO (supplier pages, grade C). I found no supplier datasheet specifically for laureth-8 sulfate, so use level and role are not documented; EWG lists the INCI (data-gap entry).
- Higher EO makes the molecule more hydrophilic, lowers surface activity, raises tolerance to hard water and electrolytes, and usually lowers viscosity build and foam stability.

### Mildness and irritation evidence
| Evidence | Result | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Alcolac patent US4412944 (full text seen, 1983; modified Draize rabbit eye test, 0.1 ml of 20% solution) | Lauryl sulfate EO0: 44.0 / 32.0 / 11.3 (days 1/2/3). Lauryl ether sulfate EO3: 40.7 / 24.0 / 5.3. Lauryl ether sulfate EO12: 0.0 / 0.0 / 0.0. C14/C16 EO6: 12.0 / 4.0 / 1.3; EO12: zero. Authors: eye irritation falls substantially as EO number rises. | B (primary data, but a patent, three rabbits per test, eye not skin; no EO8 value) |
| Same patent, foam (Ross-Miles, 0.1% active, 50 degC) | Lauryl EO3 191/167 mm (initial/5 min); lauryl EO12 166/60 mm; in 300 ppm hard water EO12 171/31 mm. | B |
| Farooq and Jayasinghe 2025, J Phys Chem B 129:10542 (abstract seen) | Simulation for SLES with 1-3 EO: electrostatic solvation more favourable with longer ethoxy spacer, consistent with lower CMC and lower skin irritation under the monomer-penetration theory. | A- (computational, covers only x = 1-3) |
| JSCC 42(2) 1991, phospholipid liposome/surfactant interactions as predictors of skin irritation (abstract page seen; authors not shown) | Liposome interaction c-value: SLS 103.0, SLES-oxo 47.8, SLES-coco 32.0, ether carboxylate (3.8 EO) 21.0; soap-chamber scores 4.75, 1.36, 0.89, 0.70. Authors note ethoxylation can raise or lower aggressiveness depending on charge/HLB. | A- (skin-relevant, but only low EO) |
| Zehnder et al. 1992, JSCC 43:313 (abstract seen) | Ether carboxylates with 4 vs 11.4 EO: the higher-EO one was better at reducing SLS irritation in a human occlusive patch study | A- (different head group, shows direction) |
| CIR amended report on sodium laureth sulfate and related salts, Int J Toxicol 2010;29 Suppl:151S-161S (abstract via search; full text blocked) | Dermal and ocular irritant, not a sensitizer; use only when formulated to be non-irritating. Severity rises with concentration. | A (abstract-level) |
| Patent claim (formulation-specific, unverified) that ethoxylation below 3 loses mildness in a sulfosuccinate/betaine system | I could not locate the passage in the Unilever patent that was cited; do not use | not usable |

Nothing found: zein, collagen swelling, TEWL or human patch data comparing 2-3 EO against 8 EO for lauryl ether sulfate.

### Cleansing and sebum removal
- Direction of evidence: more EO lowers foam stability (patent above) and, for long-chain ether sulfates below about 5 EO, one study reports decreasing CMC, Krafft point, foam and detergency with rising EO (ScienceDirect abstract via search, not opened; treat as unverified). No sebum-removal data at 8 EO.

### Mapping reasoning
- Chemistry is an alkyl ether sulfate; the standard's row is "Strong anionic, ether/sulfonate type" and C1 already treats ether sulfate plus a buffer as `moderate`. The EO number is a within-row modifier, not a new class.
- Evidence supports "less irritating and probably less foam/detergency than SLES-2/3EO", not "as mild as isethionates/amino-acid acylates", and not "harsher".
- **Recommendation:** keep in the strong anionic ether/sulfonate row; record "high-EO (8)" as a counter-signal that may lower confidence to `moderate` and name the neighbor, per the existing counter-signal convention. Do not move it to mild anionic. Do not treat it as equal to SLES-2/3EO in prose. Confidence about 0.7 for the row; high that it is not harsher than SLES.

## Cross-cutting notes for the team

1. Items 1 and 3 hinge on one manufacturer table and one in vitro preprint respectively. Both mappings should carry the N-ALT neighbor and `moderate` confidence rather than `high`.
2. Item 2 has no usable evidence. The recommended handling is a rule (never defining or reinforcing anionic), not a claim about mildness. Flag for Nick as an open call if a product's cleansing depends on it.
3. Item 4 shows the evidence ladder works in one direction only: higher EO is milder for eyes, but no data say it is a different class.
4. All four ingredients lack CIR/SCCS mildness conclusions beyond amine oxide (CIR 2008) and SLES-family (CIR 2010). No SCCS opinion found for any of them.
5. Product copy must not say "mild" for any of them on this evidence.
