# Personal Plan Leave-in evidence

## Status

External evidence has been reviewed for category ownership, market taxonomy, wash-day and heat-event application, between-wash use, product-specific protocols, and the boundary between conditioning and Styling. Current recommendation-engine behavior is documented separately in `decision.md` as an internal baseline and must not be cited as efficacy or safety evidence.

## Answer-first findings

### Leave-in has its strongest direct case for persistent post-wash manageability

**Evidence: moderate.** Dermatology guidance describes immediate reductions in frizz and easier combing, smoothing, detangling, and styling from a correctly selected Leave-in. It identifies long, curly, dry/brittle, chemically treated, heat-styled, tangled, frizzy, and flyaway hair as profiles that may benefit. Cosmetic-science reviews support the mechanism: deposited conditioning films reduce surface charge and friction, smooth the cuticle surface, reduce flyaway behavior, and improve softness and shine.

Product implication: Leave-in can be the primary category for a material post-wash detangling/manageability problem or for distributed dry/frizzy lengths that remain under-served by the baseline Conditioner. It is not automatically primary for every outcome described by the word “frizz.”

### Frizz is not one category job

**Evidence: moderate for conditioning and styling mechanisms; weak for one universal product hierarchy.** Conditioning products can reduce friction, static, flyaways, and rough-surface frizz. Fixative styling polymers instead create a film whose adhesion/cohesion determines stiffness and curl retention, including under humidity. Dermatology guidance suggests adding a small amount of oil when hair remains dry or frizzy after Leave-in, which supports Oil as a possible finishing/sealing layer rather than proof that Oil universally replaces conditioning.

Product implication: infer the likely frizz job from the rest of the lossless profile rather than asking every user a new question:

- dry, tangled, flyaway, or broadly rough lengths: Leave-in may be the best additional category after Conditioner;
- shape loss, humidity-driven expansion, or a desire for lasting curl definition/hold: Styling is the more direct owner;
- localized dry ends or final surface polish after adequate conditioning: Oil/serum may be the more direct finishing owner;
- structural roughness/damage: Conditioner and damage-care categories remain primary, with Leave-in supporting daily manageability.

### A curl cast and durable definition are styling jobs

**Evidence: moderate mechanistic evidence; limited direct consumer comparative trials.** Styling gels use fixative polymers whose film properties are measured through stiffness and high-humidity curl-retention performance. Leave-in Conditioner can improve moisture, slip, and manageability, but those functions do not establish durable hold or a cast.

Product implication: a definition goal alone does not automatically make Leave-in the best category. A Gel, Mousse, or genuine hold-providing Curl cream is the more direct category for cast, hold, and longevity. Leave-in becomes primary only when the actual unresolved job is conditioning, detangling, moisture, or manageable curl formation. A “curl cream” must be classified by verified function: conditioning without meaningful hold is Leave-in; film-forming hold is Styling; a verified hybrid may cover both.

### Curly/coily texture supports frequent usefulness, not universal necessity

**Evidence: moderate professional guidance.** Dermatologists note that curly/coily hair is more prone to dryness, breakage, knotting, and frizz and recommend full-length rinse-out conditioning. A Leave-in or Oil after washing can assist moisturization, and Leave-in is recommended for between-wash detangling. The same guidance emphasizes trial and error rather than one mandatory product stack.

Product implication: curly/coily texture alone can justify `optional` Leave-in because credible additional conditioning and manageability value exists. It becomes `basis` when the profile adds a material job such as dryness, tangling, frizz/manageability, or another confirmed Leave-in-owned need. Texture alone does not make Leave-in universally mandatory, and cast/hold still belongs to Styling.

### Shine can come from several surface-conditioning routes

**Evidence: moderate for conditioning; weak and product-specific for named oils.** Conditioning films can smooth the hair surface, reduce friction, and improve gloss/shine. Suitable cosmetic emulsions can restore some surface hydrophobicity and gloss after bleaching. Evidence for pure oils is heterogeneous: coconut oil has stronger evidence for protein-loss/damage mitigation, while a systematic review found only weak evidence for castor oil increasing luster and no significant evidence that argan oil improves hair quality.

Product implication: a shine goal alone makes Leave-in at most `optional`. If the hair is rough, dry, tangled, or frizzy, the conditioning job may make Leave-in primary and shine a co-benefit. If hair is already adequately conditioned and the desired job is immediate surface polish, Oil/serum may be more direct. Baseline Conditioner remains a legitimate shine contributor.

### The market is multi-functional, but Leave-in usually remains care plus preparation

**Evidence: strong for market positioning; not efficacy evidence.** Representative manufacturer protocols show several recurring directions: lightweight detangling sprays/milks, moisture creams, repair-support treatments, care products with explicit thermal protection, smoothing/blow-dry primers, and dedicated curl-refresh mists. Many products combine two or more jobs.

The recurring boundary is that moisture, clumping, smoothing, or the word “definition” does not establish hold. Curlsmith, for example, positions its Air Dry Cream as a Leave-in and instructs users to follow with a Styler for definition and hold. Dedicated fixative Styling remains responsible for durable cast and retention.

Product implication: model independent verified format, weight, care, heat, stage, and functional facts rather than forcing one exclusive Leave-in type. `curl_shape_support` may be a legitimate Leave-in benefit; hold is not inferred.

### Wash-day application is the general baseline; other use is event- and product-specific

**Evidence: moderate professional guidance plus direct manufacturer protocols.** AAD guidance recommends applying a small amount on damp hair where needed and notes that every-shampoo use may help relevant long or chemically treated profiles. It also cautions against scalp application and excess buildup. Some ordinary Leave-ins explicitly permit dry or between-wash application, while dedicated curl refreshers are marketed as a separate routine moment.

No credible universal “every N non-wash days” cadence emerged. Meaningful between-wash care exists, but depends on an eligible product, actual dry/frizzy/tangled lengths, and weight/buildup tolerance.

Product implication: compile post-wash Leave-in from eligible wash occurrences. Reserve between-wash refresh for the later day-type model rather than prescribing a fixed numeric cadence now.

### Heat protection is an event-specific verified capability

**Evidence: moderate professional/manufacturer guidance.** AAD guidance says Leave-in counts as Heat protection only when the package explicitly says so. Heat-tool guidance instructs protection before each heat-styling event, including later dry-hair events. A wash-day application must not be assumed to protect an unrelated later heat exposure.

Product implication: a combined product can satisfy care and the associated heat event when its verified stage/protocol permits it. Later heat exposure triggers a new `pre_heat_application`; use the same product only when dry/damp directions support that event, otherwise use a dedicated Heat protectant.

### Special treatments do not define ordinary Leave-in rules

**Evidence: strong product-specific protocol evidence.** Products such as K18 use a shampoo/no-Conditioner/waiting-period protocol and an initial wash sequence. They are treatment/Bondbuilder behavior, not evidence that ordinary Leave-in Conditioner replaces rinse-out Conditioner or follows the same cadence.

Product implication: keep verified special protocols authoritative in their actual category. Do not generalize their frequency or replacement behavior to Leave-in.

### Creator material was not sufficient for a deterministic rule

Abbey Yung has an original Leave-in application video, but accessible search/watch metadata did not expose a reliable transcript confirming non-wash guidance. Tom Hannemann/TheBeautifulPeople social content required login or was robots-blocked, and no attributable public post on ordinary Leave-in frequency could be verified. Creator snippets and third-party summaries therefore did not enter the hard category rules.

## Research questions for the category pass

External research should be commissioned only as each product-policy question becomes concrete. Likely evidence-sensitive questions are:

1. Which hair conditions and user jobs are meaningfully served by a rinse-free conditioning product rather than Conditioner, styling product, Oil, Mask, or behavior guidance?
2. When does a Leave-in materially improve post-wash detangling, friction, softness, frizz/surface control, or definition support?
3. Can a Leave-in safely replace rinse-out Conditioner in any narrow profile/product case, or should it remain additive in V1?
4. How should formula weight, format, amount, and application area vary with thickness, density, texture, length, volume sensitivity, and buildup risk?
5. Which product claims and verified protocol facts are required before a Leave-in can satisfy blow-dry, diffuser, or high-temperature direct-heat protection?
6. What conservative application guidance is defensible without exact product directions, including damp/dry stage, distribution, detangling, reapplication, and refresh use?
7. What safety, reaction, scalp-placement, buildup, breakage, repair, and split-end boundaries must be explicit?

## Evidence standard

- Prefer dermatology, cosmetic-science, hair-fibre, regulatory, and exact manufacturer protocol sources appropriate to the question.
- Keep creator technique guidance separate from efficacy and safety evidence.
- Record evidence strength and limitations; weak evidence does not become a hard deterministic rule.
- Product-specific heat temperatures, activation, amounts, and application order require a verified product source rather than category inference.
