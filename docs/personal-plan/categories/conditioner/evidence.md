# Personal Plan Conditioner evidence

## Scope

External evidence for rinse-out Conditioner relevance, cadence, product-direction inputs, application, detangling, and safety boundaries. This file does not define executable Personal Plan behavior; confirmed product decisions live in `decision.md`.

## Evidence labels

- `strong`: consistent scientific/clinical support or clear safety consensus;
- `moderate`: direct authoritative guidance or plausible evidence with limitations;
- `weak`: indirect evidence, professional practice, or mixed findings;
- `unknown`: insufficient support and unsuitable for a hard rule.

## Answer-first findings

### Rinse-out Conditioner is primarily post-shampoo length care

**Evidence: moderate.** Dermatology guidance and cosmetic-science reviews support Conditioner after cleansing to reduce friction, improve wet combability, support detangling, and temporarily improve softness/surface feel. These sources do not support permanent split-end repair or reversal of structural damage.

Product implication: Conditioner can be the regular length-care baseline after eligible Shampoo wash events, while scalp treatment, cleansing, and root oil control remain outside its role.

### Cadence can follow eligible Shampoo wash events

**Evidence: moderate.** General dermatology guidance places rinse-out Conditioner after Shampoo, while exact product timing and exceptions remain formula/protocol dependent.

Product implication: the conservative category default is one Conditioner step after the final Shampoo rinse of each eligible wash event. Double Shampooing does not automatically create two Conditioner applications. Co-wash, 2-in-1, clinician-directed, and verified product-protocol cases require explicit handling.

This evidence supports the event trigger; it does not determine how several Conditioner products should divide those events. Product allocation is an internal plan decision.

### Placement should adapt to the hair rather than follow one universal rule

**Evidence: moderate.** The American Academy of Dermatology recommends focusing Conditioner on the ends for fine/straight hair and distributing through the full hair length for dry/curly hair. A cautious general fallback is mid-lengths to ends, with deliberate scalp application avoided unless the exact product and context support it.

Product implication:

- fine or straight hair: ends-first/light placement;
- dry, curly, or coily hair: fuller length coverage;
- otherwise: mid-lengths to ends;
- oily, sensitive, itchy, or reaction-prone scalp: avoid intentional scalp application.

Hair length, density, thickness, and texture may change coverage, amount, and whether sectioning helps. They do not justify a universal amount in pumps, grams, or coin sizes.

### Exact amount and dwell time are product-specific

**Evidence: moderate for following verified directions; weak for universal amounts.** No defensible category-wide amount or multi-minute dwell rule applies across formulations and hair profiles.

Product implication: follow verified label/protocol data. Without it, start with the minimum needed for even coverage, add incrementally, and avoid fabricated numerical precision. Rinse-out Conditioner should be rinsed thoroughly rather than intentionally left behind.

### Conditioner can support gentle in-shower detangling

**Evidence: moderate to strong.** Dermatology guidance supports gentle ends-first detangling and, particularly for thick/curly hair, use of a wide-tooth comb while Conditioner is present. Wet hair remains mechanically vulnerable, so forceful repeated combing is inappropriate.

Product implication: Conditioner provides baseline slip, but a larger persistent detangling need may still make Leave-in the stronger additional lever. Do not turn detangling into a separate Conditioner product-fit axis without supporting product data.

### Sectioning is conditional coverage guidance

**Evidence: weak to moderate.** Sectioning is useful professional practice for long, dense, thick, curly, coily, or tangle-prone hair when it materially improves even coverage. Evidence does not support one mandatory number of sections.

### Repair level, weight, and care direction are different decisions

**Evidence: mixed.** Hair diameter and weight sensitivity are practical formula-weight signals. Surface condition, chemical treatment, breakage, and damage support a repair-intensity direction. Home elasticity observations may be retained as a contextual heuristic for moisture/protein direction, but they do not diagnose a proven deficiency.

Product implication: a light Conditioner can still have high repair support. Do not automatically make Conditioner richer because repair need is high, and contextualize elasticity with other damage/dryness inputs.

## Abbey Yung technique review

The verified primary source is Abbey Yung's video “How to Apply Conditioner for WAY Better Results (The Abbey Yung Method Hacks: Step 6).” A full platform transcript was not available during review, so reconstructed steps must not be presented as exact quotations.

Evidence-aligned or reasonable optional elements:

- apply with even coverage, commonly ends-first;
- divide hair into manageable sections when helpful;
- distribute product between the hands before application;
- detangle gently from the ends upward;
- follow product directions and rinse.

Do not encode as universal rules:

- exactly two sections;
- a fixed amount or dwell time;
- “emulsifying” a finished Conditioner as a required performance mechanism;
- universal mask-then-Conditioner layering;
- a cold rinse “sealing” the cuticle;
- structural-repair claims from rinse-out Conditioner.

## Safety boundary

Conditioner-associated burning, itching, rash, swelling, or persistent scalp symptoms should stop product optimization. The conservative response is to stop the suspected product and seek professional assessment when symptoms are severe, unusual, or persistent. Do not diagnose the cause.

## Open evidence gaps

- No strong universal dose rule by length, density, thickness, or texture.
- No universal Conditioner dwell time independent of product directions.
- No evidence that a cold final rinse permanently seals the cuticle.
- Limited evidence for exact product-level protein/moisture classification without verified formulation metadata.
